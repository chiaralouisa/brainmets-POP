Metachronous Brain Metastases in NSCLC Show Markedly Worse Post-Metastasis Survival Despite a Shared Genomic Landscape

L. Hempel, M. Sarwary, L. Fabregas-Ibanez, N. Miglino, M. Nowak, S. Rahmani Khajouei, M. Zoche, A. Wicki, L. Boos

Department of Medical Oncology and Hematology, University Hospital Zurich · University of Zurich, Switzerland

Background

Brain metastases (BM) develop in up to 40% of patients with NSCLC and remain a dominant cause of death. Patients are conventionally split into those presenting with BM at diagnosis (synchronous, sBM) and those developing BM later (metachronous, mBM).

The distinction guides surveillance and local therapy, yet its biological basis is unclear, and comparative survival data are hard to interpret.

The measurement problem

Studies almost always measure overall survival (OS) from primary diagnosis. A patient cannot enter the mBM group without first surviving long enough for BM to appear — that interval is immortal time, and it is unavailable to the sBM group.

We asked: does the choice of time origin change the direction of the sBM/mBM comparison, and do the two groups differ genomically?

Study Design

116 patients, NSCLC + confirmed BM
88 synchronous BM (≤3 months)
28 metachronous BM (>3 months)

Monocentric retrospective cohort, University Hospital Zurich; consecutive patients, all histologic subtypes.

Comprehensive genomic profiling by FoundationOne; PD-L1 by VENTANA SP263.

Kaplan–Meier with log-rank; Cox regression. Genomic comparisons by Fisher's exact test with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR correction.

9p21.3 co-deletion frequency benchmarked against published rates in unselected NSCLC on comparable CGP.

Endpoints

Primary: OS from brain-metastasis diagnosis.
Secondary: OS from primary diagnosis — reported side by side so the two clocks can be compared directly.

Summary

The sBM/mBM survival comparison reverses with the time origin: no difference from primary diagnosis (p = 0.47), roughly three-fold hazard in mBM from BM diagnosis (p < 0.001).

The difference arises entirely from the metachronous group, whose median more than halves once the interval preceding BM is removed.

No alteration distinguishes the groups after multiplicity correction; co-alteration patterns are similar.

9p21.3 co-deletion is frequent, but neither enriched nor prognostic — frequency alone is not evidence of enrichment.

Conclusion

Anchored to BM diagnosis, the apparent advantage of metachronous disease reverses. mBM carries almost three times the hazard, consistent with BM arising in pretreated, therapy-resistant disease.

The shared genomic landscape suggests brain-metastatic competence is established at or before primary diagnosis, with BM timing reflecting treatment and surveillance rather than a distinct metastatic genotype.

9p21.3 co-deletion nonetheless defines a therapeutically addressable subgroup (MTAP loss; PRMT5/MAT2A inhibition), independent of BM timing.

Studies comparing synchronous and metachronous brain metastases should report survival from the metastatic event, not only from primary diagnosis.

Limitations

Single centre, retrospective; 28 mBM patients. No BM-free comparator arm, so no statement about BM risk is made. CNS-directed therapy (SRS, WBRT, resection) not captured as a discrete variable. In the 72 patients with complete clinical annotation the effect attenuates to HR 1.85 (1.07–3.21).

Kaplan–Meier medians and Cox models computed in Python (statsmodels); patient-level, de-duplicated alteration calls; two-sided tests throughout.
